## Why the spreadsheet becomes a bottleneck

Excel is often an SMB’s first system. It’s flexible, everyone knows how to open it and it needs no installation. That is exactly why it ends up carrying everything: the client list, quote tracking, inventory, billable hours, the Monday report.

The problem isn’t the tool, it’s the role it’s given. A spreadsheet is designed to calculate and analyze. It isn’t designed to serve as a shared database, a tracking system or a hand-off point between several teams. When it plays those roles, four kinds of friction appear.

- **Multiple versions.** The file “client-tracking-FINAL-v3.xlsx” circulates by email, next to two other copies. No one knows which one is the reference.
- **Re-entry.** The same information is typed into the spreadsheet, then into the accounting software, then into a confirmation email. Every copy is a chance for error.
- **Silent errors.** An overwritten formula, a sort applied to a single column, a row deleted by mistake: nothing warns you, and the error makes its way into the report.
- **Access.** The file is shared with everyone, or with no one. It’s hard to know who changed what, and even harder to remove access when someone leaves the team.

## Signs it’s time to move on

There is no universal threshold. Some signals do come up often, though:

- several people need to edit the same file at the same time;
- you copy data from one file to another every week;
- the file is slow to open, or it closes without warning;
- only one person really understands how it works, and their absence blocks the team;
- your reports are built by hand and already out of date when you present them;
- personal information about clients or members circulates as attachments;
- you hesitate to make a decision because you doubt the numbers.

If several of these statements sound familiar, the spreadsheet is no longer saving you time: it’s costing you. To get a sense of how much, the [re-entry cost calculator](/en/tools/re-entry-calculator/) gives an estimate based on your own figures.

## A six-step method

### 1. Take inventory of your files

List the spreadsheets that really matter: the ones opened every week and the ones an invoice, a payroll run or a decision depends on. For each, note who fills it in, who reads it, where the data comes from and where it goes next. This inventory often reveals duplicates: two teams keeping the same list, each in its own way.

### 2. Identify the source of truth

For each type of information (clients, projects, invoices, inventory), decide where the reference version lives. Just one. The other places become copies fed automatically, or they disappear. This decision matters more than the choice of software: without it, you will simply move the mess into a new tool.

### 3. Clean up before you move

Remove duplicates, standardize formats (dates, phone numbers, organization names), complete the essential fields and archive what is no longer used. Take the opportunity to remove personal information you no longer need. Migrating dirty data gives you a new tool that no one trusts.

### 4. Choose the tool after the process

First describe the process: who does what, in what order, with which exceptions. Only then look for the tool that supports it. You may already have, among your current subscriptions, an application able to take over. The reverse, buying software and then adapting the team to its screens, often leads to a second spreadsheet kept in parallel “just in case.” That is why we don’t resell software: we start from your processes, then choose and connect the right tools, including the ones you already have.

### 5. Migrate in small batches

Don’t switch everything at once. Start with a single process, ideally the one that causes the most re-entry. Run the old and the new side by side long enough to check the results, then retire the file. Each successful batch makes the next one easier, and the team sees a concrete gain from the first one.

### 6. Train and document

A tool no one has mastered sends everyone back to Excel. Plan a short training session for each role, simple documentation (what to enter where, whom to ask for help) and a follow-up check after the first weeks of use. Finally, name one person responsible for each source of truth.

## What stays in Excel

Moving off Excel doesn’t mean giving it up. The spreadsheet remains an excellent tool for:

- one-off analysis and scenarios (“what if we revised our prices?”);
- financial models and working budgets;
- exploring data exported from a reliable source;
- prototypes, to test an idea before building it into a system.

The rule is simple: Excel can read the source of truth, it should no longer be it.
